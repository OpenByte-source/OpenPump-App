We wrote our own app to run our pump the way we wanted.

The app that came with it only let us do what its makers had thought of. We wanted to find out what the hardware itself could do, so we built one that hands the settings to you.

Why bother

The only way in

Most smart pumps come with an app from the company that made them, and that app is usually the only way in. For now OpenPump runs one pump, the Epic Hydro PE Pump. The goal is any Bluetooth pump whose protocol can be reverse engineered.

Routines and records should belong to whoever's using the pump.

Changing it mid-run

Pull, hold, drop and drop time change from the − / + controls while a set is running, and More › has the speed. Coming steps changes the steps still to come. It all applies to this run only: when it's over, you decide whether the saved routine keeps the change.

What never changes

Anything above your ceiling is lowered before it reaches the pump. A session ends and vents on its own after two hours under pressure.

2.1–16.8inHg (7–57 kPa): the ceiling, set by you

Each step is one press of the app's + or −: the ceiling moves one notch.

When you want it

Kept to yourself

Settings › Privacy has a switch for each incognito feature — a disguised icon and name (Fitness log, Habits or Notes, worn on the app lock too), discreet notifications, hiding from recent apps, quick hide — plus one that turns your chosen set on and off together, off until you turn it on. STOP stays reachable, and safety alerts still ring and vibrate.

Start here

Your first session

  1. Try every screen on the simulated pump: Settings › Device & developer. No hardware needed.
  2. Connect your Epic Hydro PE Pump.
  3. Pick a routine on Today.
  4. Press start. Simulated runs are always labelled.

New versions go up on the Releases page. Follow the project on GitHub to hear when one does.

Kinds of set

Early on we noticed that a set can take almost any shape, and the same shape feels different from one person to the next. So instead of shipping a list of presets, we broke a set down into the numbers it's made of and handed all of them over.

fixed · the same pull every rep

ramp · 2 to 9 steps from one pressure to another

rest · the pump vents and a timer runs

Pick a kind, then set the numbers: how hard it pulls, how long it holds, how far it lets go and for how long, and how fast the pump works. Switch on per-rep edits in Settings and each rep of a fixed set can have its own values as well.

People shape their sessions around their own comfort and preferences, and around anything they'd rather avoid. The app doesn't decide any of that for you.

Pull to −4.1 inHg and hold for 40 seconds, drop for 8, and repeat for 4 minutes.

Keeping track is the hard part

Following a long-term plan means remembering what you ran and how it went, then matching that against where the plan says you should be by now. That's where people lose the thread, or end up buried in numbers.

So the training plan does that part. You tell it how long your sessions run now and the pressure you use, then pick the days you can train. It starts each track where its own session is closest to yours. You train. As your sessions come in, it adjusts the routine it wrote for you, plans the deload weeks, and tells you when you've met the conditions for the next level. A week counts with two full sessions of a track, or three shorter ones. Moving up is your call, at your own pace: while your measurements are on target and rising, its scheduled steps come half as often, and the card asks whether to step up now or wait.

It applies publicly available training guidance mechanically. Every decision shows its reason, and whether the rule behind it is stated in a source or was worked out. It can't know your body, and it isn't advice. It stays off until you turn it on. It never stops you starting a session, and it leaves any routine you've changed yourself alone.

Measurements only compare when they're taken the same way

Any way of measuring works, as long as you keep using the same one. The app keeps every measurement with the way it was taken and with the session around it, and it only lines a reading up against others taken the same way.

A reading taken under pressure is stored with the pressure the pump actually reported, and it's only compared with another taken within 0.3 inHg (1 kPa) of it. So you can stop guessing whether two numbers belong on the same line.

    For developers

    How it's put together, in enough detail to find your way around the source.

    The main parts

    SessionActivity
    The launcher and the run engine. It's still over 41,000 lines, although the screens have moved into classes of their own: RunScreen, LibraryScreen, ProgressScreen, TrainerScreen, SettingsScreen and a few more.
    PumpLink
    The Bluetooth link, which scans, connects, writes frames and reads the pump's telemetry. PumpMatch picks the pump out of a scan, Proto builds and parses the frames, and Conn tracks the connection state.
    SimPump
    A model of the pump's firmware. A switch in Settings (Device & developer › Simulated pump) swaps it in inside PumpLink, so the whole app runs with no hardware, and every run it files is marked simulated.
    Model and Store
    Sets, stages, routines, sessions and readings as plain data classes with their JSON. Store keeps them in one file on the phone.
    Session and AsRun
    Session makes the run's decisions without touching Android. AsRun records what a run actually did. RunService keeps a live run going behind a foreground notification.
    Plan and Mint
    Plan holds the training plan's progression rules, and Mint turns a prescription into sets.
    Export and Backup
    Export writes the CSV files and ExportScreen the PDF report. Backup builds and checks the zip.

    Building and testing

    git clone https://github.com/OpenByte-source/OpenPump-App.git
cd OpenPump-App
./gradlew test                 # every check, no phone needed
./gradlew :app:assembleDebug   # the APK
./gradlew installDebug         # onto a connected phone or an emulator

    You need JDK 17 or newer and the Android SDK, platform 34. Android Studio installs both.

    A Gradle build with no third-party libraries in the app. It targets SDK 34 and runs on Android 7 (SDK 24) and up. The logic lives in a plain Java 17 module, 126 files that never import Android, so most of it is tested on any computer without an emulator.

    What the exports look like

    sessions.csv

    One row per session: when, which routine, how long, the peak and the dose, the change in length and girth, and how it felt.

    readings.csv

    One row per measurement: when, how it was measured, length and girth, the hold it was taken under, and which photos you took.

    • The headers never change without a new version; the self-test checks them.

    What a pump driver would have to do

    match
    Recognise the pump from the name it advertises.
    write
    Send a preset and get an acknowledgement back.
    listen
    Report samples with a unit and a rate, and say plainly when there's no measurement.
    stop
    Vent, and report the pressure falling so the app can confirm it.

    None of that exists as an interface yet. The only boundary today is PumpLink's listener callback, and pulling a driver interface out of PumpLink is the first job on the list after the release.
